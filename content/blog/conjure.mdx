I want to write more poetry. The problem is that most of what I write is bad, and bad poems tend to die in a notes app where nobody, including me, ever looks at them again.

So I built myself a reward. It’s called [Conjure](https://conjure-inky.vercel.app/). You write a poem, press Submit, and it paints you a picture of it. Good poem, bad poem, a poem about toast: you still get a picture. The point isn’t to write something great. The point is to write something, and to get a little hit of joy for showing up.

## The rules

There are only two, and they’re both about making it easy to write badly.

- **Your poem isn’t saved.** It disappears the moment the picture appears. It isn’t stored or sent anywhere, so there’s nothing to be embarrassed about later.
- **The picture is yours.** You can flip it to grayscale and download it. That’s it. No account, no feed, no likes.

The picture is the only thing that survives. Which means the reward for writing a poem is a strange little piece of art that exists nowhere else.

## The setup

Same three tools I used to build this site:

- **Claude** wrote the code. I described the feeling I wanted, looked at what came back and kept pushing it somewhere weirder.
- **GitHub** holds the code. Every change is a commit I can look back on.
- **Vercel** hosts it. Every push to the main branch redeploys the site.

Conjure itself is about as simple as a website gets: plain HTML, CSS and a canvas. No framework, no build step and no server. Everything happens in your browser, which is also why your poem never has to go anywhere.

I built most of it in one day. The commit log is mostly me tweaking how it looks: softer palettes, a faster reveal, then a grungier one, then a more jarring one, then ASCII.

## Inspired by Zach Lieberman

A lot of the spirit behind Conjure comes from [Zach Lieberman](https://zach.li). He’s an artist who writes code to make poetic, playful visuals, co-created the openFrameworks toolkit and co-founded the School for Poetic Computation. He’s also known for posting a new sketch almost every day, for years.

That habit is what gets me. Not every sketch is a masterpiece, and that’s the point. You show up, you make something small, you put it out there, and you do it again tomorrow. Conjure is my version of that for poetry, with code doing the drawing.

## How a poem becomes a picture

Conjure doesn’t use AI to make the pictures. Claude helped write the code, but the art itself comes from a set of rules that measure your poem and turn those measurements into choices. It works in two steps.

### Step 1: read the poem

First, Conjure reads the poem and turns it into a list of numbers, most of them on a scale from 0 to 1. It looks at:

- **Sound.** What share of the letters are hard consonants (p, b, t, d, k, g), hissing ones (s, z, sh), liquid ones (l, r) or humming ones (m, n), compared to typical English.
- **Rhythm.** How many syllables each line has, and how steady that count is. Steady lines read as meter. It also counts stanzas, line lengths and how many lines run on without punctuation.
- **Rhyme.** It compares the endings of nearby lines. A full rhyme counts as one, a slant rhyme (same final consonants) counts as a half.
- **Punctuation.** Exclamation points, question marks, commas, dashes and ellipses, per line.
- **Repetition and alliteration.** How often you repeat yourself, and how often neighboring words start with the same sound.
- **Feeling.** It counts words from a short list of warm ones (love, light, bloom, home) and heavy ones (dark, cold, ache, gone) to get a mood from negative to positive, and how intense it is.
- **Imagery.** It checks for words about water, fire, earth, air, night, the body, time and love. Color words like “gold” or “sea” get noted too.
- **Pronouns.** How much “I,” how much “you” and how much “we.”

From those, it works out a few bigger moods: **energy** (exclamation points, hard consonants, short lines), **calm** (soft sounds, steady meter, longer lines) and **openness** (questions, trailing dots and dashes).

### Step 2: paint it

Then those numbers make the decisions.

**The scene.** Every poem becomes one of three scenes, scored against the poem:

- A **color field** that melts and warps, with one bloom per stanza. Feeling words, liquid sounds, love, the body and run-on lines pull toward this one.
- A **landscape** of dithered towers over a lake, fading into fog. Earth, water, air, calm and steady meter pull toward it.
- **ASCII**, where the picture is drawn out of typed characters. Repetition, steady meter, commas, time and hard consonants pull toward it.

**The pixel size.** Hard consonants make chunky pixels. Soft sounds make fine ones. A poem full of “k” and “t” gets big blocky squares; a poem full of “s” and “l” gets something closer to a fine print.

**The palette.** There are 25 color palettes, mostly soft and light, each tagged with the imagery it suits. Water, fire, night and so on nudge the pick toward palettes that match. About 1 in 5 pictures also gets its colors shifted, borrowed from another palette, or given an extra color.

**The details.** Smaller rules layer on top:

- Rhymes become a web of thin threads.
- Questions draw thin rings.
- Commas scatter dust.
- Dashes and energy draw zigzag lines.
- “I” becomes a slatted sun, and “you” becomes a dashed orbit.
- Sad words, fire and night make the picture melt. Flowing lines and s-sounds smear it. Calm, soft poems blur it.

**The grit.** Finally, the picture gets roughed up. Grain, color splits, glitchy rows, and on most pieces a photocopier-style grunge pass with fold creases, film scratches, dust and burnt edges. Darker poems get dirtier.

**Breaking the rules.** About 1 in 10 pictures ignores all of the above on purpose. Any scene, any palette, every effect pushed to an extreme, sometimes a mirror fold or a second scene spliced in along a jagged edge. A bad poem deserves a chance at a wild picture too.

### Why it’s never the same twice

Every poem also gets a random number mixed in before it’s painted. The poem decides the general direction, and the random number decides the specifics. Submit the same poem twice and you’ll get two pictures that feel related but aren’t the same. That also means every picture is a one-off. Once you close it, it’s gone, just like the poem.

## The takeaway

I didn’t need a tool to make me a better poet. I needed a reason to write the bad poems so the better ones have a chance to show up. A picture at the end turns out to be a pretty good reason.

[Try Conjure](https://conjure-inky.vercel.app/). Write something bad. You still get a picture.
