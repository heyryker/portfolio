For a few years, my portfolio lived on Squarespace. It did its job. It also looked a lot like every other portfolio on Squarespace, and it cost me $270 a year plus tax for the privilege.

Then someone told me I should be using AI to be more creative. I knew they had a point. My site needed an update, and I’d known it for a while. But every time I thought about rebuilding it, I pictured a heavy lift, the kind of project that eats every free evening for a month, so I kept putting it off. The ideas were never the hard part. It was everything between the idea and a working website.

So on a Saturday morning, I tried it.

## The setup

The whole stack is three tools:

- **Claude** wrote the code. I described what I wanted, looked at what came back and said what to change.
- **GitHub** holds the code. Every change lands as a pull request I can review before it goes anywhere.
- **Vercel** builds and hosts the site. Every pull request gets its own preview link, so I could click around the real thing on my laptop and my phone before saying yes.

That loop is the part that surprised me. It didn’t feel like prompting a chatbot. It felt like art directing a very fast developer who never got tired of “move it one grid cell to the left.”

## Saturday to Sunday

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/saturday.webp" width={1486} height={935} alt="An early version of the landing page on Saturday: the HEY [I’M] RYKER headline on a visible gray grid, five meeples wandering around it, two pixel flowers already planted, and the case study links in gray brackets along the bottom." caption="Saturday. The grid lines were still visible, and the meeples had already started gardening." />

I started with a brief: who the site is for, the colors, the type, a mockup of the landing page and a long description of five tiny pixel characters I wanted living on it. Then we went down the list.

By Saturday afternoon there was a landing page with a headline locked to a grid and five meeples you could drag, throw and explode. By Saturday night they had personalities, and some of them had opinions about the headline. Sunday was case studies, a blog, an About page, a menu, search and share basics, analytics and moving my domain over.

About 24 hours after I started, I had a mostly complete site and 28 merged pull requests. I’m still tweaking things, but that’s the fun part.

## The cost

Here’s the math that made me laugh:

- **Squarespace:** $270 a year, billed annually, plus tax.
- **This site:** a $20 a month Claude subscription. GitHub and Vercel are both on their free plans.

And I didn’t trade a template for a template. I got a site that does exactly what I want, down to the pixel, with nothing I didn’t ask for.

## Making it fun (and a little random)

A portfolio is supposed to be professional, and the case studies are. But I wanted the rest of the site to feel like somewhere you’d poke around for a minute longer than you meant to.

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/garden.webp" width={2400} height={1500} alt="The first heyryker.com landing page: the big HEY [I’M] RYKER headline with five small pixel meeples wandering over it, pixel flowers and grass planted around them, and the [I’M] tinted yellow where the yellow meeple is standing on it." caption="The weekend version. Five coworkers and a garden." />

That’s the meeples’ job. Each one has a personality. The yellow one is curious and follows your cursor. The green one is shy and runs away. The purple one watches. The red one is chaos. The blue one is mostly asleep, in a nightcap, and occasionally sleepwalks somewhere new. In that first version, if one walked over the blue parts of my name, it took on that meeple’s color. Click one and it explodes into pixels, then slowly puts itself back together.

None of that was in a plan. A lot of it came from me saying “what if they did this” halfway through something else. Having the code be fast and cheap to change meant I could say yes to the random ideas instead of saving them for a someday that never comes.

## Why the meeples garden

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/village.webp" width={2400} height={1500} alt="The meeple village: five small houses in each meeple’s color, a town hall with a blue roof, a fenced garden in the middle full of flowers and carrots, a justice building at the bottom, and cows, sheep and bunnies in the grass." caption="The village. Yes, there’s a courthouse." />

Outside of design, I love gardening and landscaping. There’s something about planning a space, putting things in the ground and watching it fill in over time that scratches the same itch as building a brand.

So the meeples garden too. In the first version, if you left the landing page open they’d plant flowers, grass and carrots around the headline. The red one trampled them, because of course it did. Click the period after my name and a gold flower popped up, and all five of them gathered round to cheer.

They even have a village now, with houses, a big shared garden in the middle and a few cows, sheep and bunnies. It has absolutely nothing to do with my resume, and it might be my favorite page on the site.

## Then I kept going

The weekend version was fun. It was also loud. A big headline, a garden, five characters and a blue accent all asking for attention at once. When I looked at it as a hiring manager would, the work was the quietest thing on the page.

That bugged me, because it isn’t how I design. I believe in Swiss design: a clear grid, one good typeface, lots of white space and nothing on the page that isn’t doing a job. The personality should be in the details, not shouting over the work. So I ran two more rounds, each one taking something away.

### Round one: black, white and one yellow

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/landing-serif.webp" width={2400} height={1500} alt="The second version of the landing page: a large Times serif headline reading Hi, I’m Ryker, a short line about building brand systems, Based in Utah in yellow italics, underlined Resume and Email me links, and below them a 3D stack of case study cards angled away from the viewer, starting with the SixFifty editorial report." caption="Round one. A serif hello and a stack of case studies you could flip through." />

I dropped the blue and moved to black and white with a single yellow accent, used as a highlight and never as text. The pixel font gave way to a Times serif. The meeples moved out to their own village, and the case studies became a stack of cards you could flip through.

It felt more grown up, but the stack was still the show. You had to play with it to find the work.

### Round two: a grid, a clock and gray cards

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/project-view.webp" width={2400} height={1500} alt="The current project view: on the left, my name, the live Utah date and time and the list of gray project cards with TRON DAO highlighted; on the right, a large full-bleed image of the TRON brand guidelines site on a desktop monitor, arrows to step through the images, and the case study title and summary in a gray panel underneath." caption="Round two. Everything sits in two columns, and the work gets the big side." />

For the latest version I took a lot of cues from the website of [Porto Rocha](https://portorocha.com), the design studio. It’s about as simple as a portfolio gets: a name, a little context and the work in plain cards. Credit where it’s due, it’s the reference I showed Claude.

My name now sits at the top in a plain uppercase sans serif. Under it is the date and time in Utah, ticking, with today’s weather in gray. It’s a small thing, but it tells you I’m a real person somewhere, and that it’s probably snowing. Below that are gray cards for every case study and post, all the same size, all on the same grid. Open one and the work takes the big half of the screen, with the list still there on the left.

### Where the personality went

<Figure src="/blog/building-this-site/meeples-released.webp" width={2400} height={1500} alt="The current landing page mid-mischief: the purple meeple in a top hat singing LA LA LAAA above my name, the yellow one lassoing the Resume button, the blue one napping in a nightcap, the red one with horns, and the green one planting a trail of pixel flowers down the right side of the page." caption="Press the little button in the corner and you get about twelve seconds of this." />

I didn’t want to lose the fun, just put it where it belongs. So the meeples live behind a small button in the bottom corner. Press it and they burst onto the page and get to work: the purple one sings, the yellow one lassos my Resume button, the green one plants flowers, the red one shoves cards around and the blue one naps. After a few seconds a pixel cop car drives in, sirens going, and picks them all up.

That’s the balance I was after. A calm, clean page that gets out of the way of the work, with a little bit of me waiting for anyone who goes looking. Each round took minutes to try instead of weeks, which is the only reason I was willing to throw out a version I already liked.

## The takeaway

The advice was right, just not how I expected. AI didn’t give me ideas. It took away the cost of trying them. When a weird idea takes minutes to see instead of a weekend, you try a lot more weird ideas, and some of them turn into tiny pixel people with a vegetable garden. It also makes it cheap to take things away, which turned out to matter just as much.

If you’ve been sitting on a site you’re not excited about, this is your sign. Your move.
